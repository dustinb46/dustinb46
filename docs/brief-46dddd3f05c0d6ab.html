01 · Trade

White House lets USMCA lapse into review — putting Canada's dairy barriers back on the table

On July 2, U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer confirmed the administration will not renew the USMCA in its current form; the pact stays in force but shifts into a rolling joint-review process, and dairy groups are pressing to use it to pry open Canada's under-filled tariff-rate quotas. Why it matters: more than 40% of U.S. dairy exports by value go to Mexico and Canada, and a live renegotiation is the strongest leverage in years to fix quota access that U.S. exporters have historically filled at barely 40%.

02 · Animal Health

Texas asks the FDA to clear feed-based ivermectin as screwworm creeps north

Texas Agriculture Commissioner Sid Miller petitioned the FDA on July 1–2 to authorize ivermectin in medicated feed and mineral supplements for cattle, arguing the recently cleared injectable route can't practically dose whole herds; USDA counts 29 U.S. New World screwworm cases so far, all in Texas and New Mexico. Why it matters: NWS strikes fresh wounds and newborn-calf navels, so a feed- or mineral-delivered treatment would let operations protect entire groups without handling every animal individually — if the FDA acts.

03 · Herd

Replacement heifers keep shrinking — CoBank sees no relief until 2027

CoBank's Knowledge Exchange projects the U.S. dairy replacement-heifer pool — already at its lowest since 1978 — will tighten further in 2026, with roughly 439,000 fewer heifers entering herds this year before a partial rebound begins in 2027, the delayed echo of dairies breeding so many cows to beef. Why it matters: a thin heifer supply means costly replacements, less culling flexibility, and slower expansion even as milk prices invite it — and it's locked in for another two calving cycles.

04 · Markets

USDA opens up to $500M for small beef processors — a subsidy aimed at your beef check

On June 30 USDA launched the SPUR program (Strengthening Processing for U.S. Ranchers), offering up to $500 million in temporary payments to U.S.-owned small and mid-size beef processors squeezed by record-high cattle costs in a market where four packers control roughly 85% of slaughter. Why it matters: with beef-cross calves now driving dairy net margins more than the milk check, healthier independent beef-processing capacity helps hold up the calf prices that have become a core dairy revenue line.

05 · Inputs

Bayer wants tariffs on Chinese glyphosate — grain groups are fighting it

Bayer's Monsanto and its new glyphosate subsidiary Ruveon filed a late-June petition seeking antidumping and countervailing duties on Chinese glyphosate, alleging dumping margins from 69% to 446%; Commerce is expected to decide whether to open the case by about July 20. Why it matters: glyphosate is the workhorse burndown across corn-silage and no-till feed ground, and duties on the dominant import source would raise a basic input cost for the crops that set dairy feed bills.

06 · Inputs

USDA puts $500M toward domestic fertilizer and suspends Moroccan phosphate duties

USDA opened its $500 million FIELDS program on July 1 to fund new and expanded U.S. fertilizer plants (awards of $15M–$150M, applications due August 15), paired with a temporary suspension of countervailing duties on phosphate imported from Morocco to ease near-term prices. Why it matters: fertilizer is a top swing cost for home-raised forages and grain, and both moves aim to pull nutrient prices down — though the plant-building payoff is years out and the duty relief is the piece that lands this season.

07 · Labeling

Processor fined for calling reconstituted milk "fresh" — a labeling line regulators are policing

Australia's competition regulator, the ACCC, penalized dairy giant Lactalis A$59,400 over two milk lines marketed as "fresh" that in fact contained substantial reconstituted skim-milk powder and lactose; Lactalis has since dropped "fresh" from the labels. Why it matters: as reconstituted and plant-based products crowd the dairy case, "fresh" and origin claims are becoming an enforcement target — a signal that label integrity is now a competitive and legal front for real fluid milk.

08 · Ag-Tech

A Russian startup is implanting brain chips in cows to lift milk — with little proof and loud welfare alarms

Neiry, a Moscow neurotech firm, says it surgically implanted brain-stimulation devices in five cows ("Project Horn") to drive appetite, cut stress and raise output, claiming a 100% survival rate; neuroethicists counter that there is no published safety or efficacy data and that comparable animal brain-chip programs have killed or paralyzed test animals. Why it matters: it's a fringe experiment today, but it marks how far "productivity tech" may reach into the animal itself — and the welfare, consumer-trust and regulatory backlash any such tool would meet in Western milk markets.
