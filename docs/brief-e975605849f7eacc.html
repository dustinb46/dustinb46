01 · Trade

Washington sets a 50% tariff on Canadian whey, milk powder and protein — cheese and butter exempt

A July 20 presidential proclamation, published in the Federal Register on July 23, invokes Section 338 of the 1930 Tariff Act to add a 50% duty on roughly 50 lines of Canadian dairy imports — whey and whey protein, milk protein concentrates, lactose, casein and milk powders — for goods entered on or after August 19. Cheese, butter, yogurt and fluid milk are exempt, and USMCA origin provides no relief. Why it matters: The U.S. is a net whey exporter and brings in only about 21 million pounds of liquid whey a year (roughly 98% from Canada), so the direct volume hit is small — but it raises input costs for U.S. protein and ingredient processors and hands domestic whey and lactose suppliers a pricing edge.

USDA's advanced-price announcement on July 22 set the August Class I base at $18.76 per hundredweight, down $2.57 from July, with the decline driven by ample milk supplies and softening cheese values. 03 · Processing

Cheese stocks build against the seasonal norm as spot cheese and powder slide

USDA's June Cold Storage report put natural cheese inventories at 1.433 billion pounds — up 16.2 million pounds (+1.1%) from May, a month that normally sees a roughly 5.4-million-pound draw — while CME spot blocks fell to $1.6075 and nonfat dry milk slid to its lowest since January. Butter stocks, by contrast, were tight at 332 million pounds, down 6.6% year over year. Why it matters: Cheese accumulating faster than the seasonal pattern is bearish for Class III and the cheese-heavy Upper Midwest milk check, even as firm butter keeps a floor under Class IV.

04 · Supply

June milk output up 2.3% as the herd hits a 30-plus-year high

USDA's Milk Production report on July 22 pegged June output at 19.7 billion pounds, up 2.3% year over year, with the milking herd at 9.68 million head — up 192,000 in a year and the largest since the early 1990s. The July Cattle report showed dairy replacement heifers at 3.60 million head, still roughly 600,000 below a decade ago. Why it matters: Relentless supply growth is the core weight on 2026 prices, but the thin heifer pipeline caps how fast the herd can keep expanding — a brake that could tighten milk supplies later even as the market is oversupplied now.

Feed costs climb as Black Sea shipping disruption rallies grain

Corn and soybeans pushed higher the week of July 24 — December corn added about 20 cents — as attacks on Black Sea ports halted Ukrainian grain loadings and a hot, dry U.S. forecast stressed developing crops. Why it matters: Feed is the other half of the margin equation: a sustained grain rally erodes the Dairy Margin Coverage margin just as milk prices soften, squeezing the spread from both ends and strengthening the case to lock in feed coverage.

06 · Cyber

Ransomware halts Fairlife production; attackers threaten a 1TB data leak

Coca-Cola disclosed on July 16 that a ransomware attack disrupted its Fairlife dairy subsidiary and forced a production halt at U.S. facilities. On July 21–22 the Anubis group claimed it had encrypted Fairlife's systems and stolen about one terabyte of data, threatening to publish it; Canadian operations continued and product safety was said to be unaffected. Why it matters: An unplanned shutdown at a major fluid-milk and protein buyer can back milk up onto its suppliers with little notice, and the attack is a pointed reminder that processor IT outages are now a supply-chain risk dairies have to plan around.

07 · Animal Health

A Utah county declares an emergency as H5N1 slashes dairy output

Weber County, Utah declared a local state of emergency after bird flu hit four of its nine dairies, with affected farms losing 6,000 to 10,000 pounds of milk a day and 22 dairies now quarantined across Cache, Box Elder and Weber counties. Nationally, H5N1 has reached more than 1,166 herds in 19 states, with Idaho leading at 173 as the virus spreads cow-to-cow on shared equipment and labor. Why it matters: The active vector is shared equipment and crews moving between farms, not wild birds — tightening biosecurity on trucks, tools and workers is now the most direct defense against a weeks-long hit to production and fertility.
