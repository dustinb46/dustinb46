Daily Edition · Tuesday, July 28, 2026

The supply story hardened this week: June milk ran 2.4% hotter and USDA trimmed its 2026 price outlook, yet tight butter stocks and a turn higher at the global auction complicate the bearish read. In Washington, the southern cattle border gets a reopening date and farm inputs dodge new tariffs.

01 · Trade & Animal Health

USDA sets an August 24 restart for cattle imports through the southern border

USDA announced July 24 a phased reopening of southern livestock ports that have been closed for more than a year over the New World screwworm, beginning with Douglas, Arizona on August 24 and then evaluating Santa Teresa and Columbus, New Mexico; every animal will undergo a full USDA screwworm inspection, and the reopening is contingent on Mexico adhering to the Joint Action Plan. Why it matters: renewed Mexican feeder-cattle flow relieves the tight North American cattle supply that has propped up cull-cow and beef-on-dairy calf values — while reopening a screwworm exposure pathway every operation should track.

02 · Milk Price

USDA cuts its 2026 milk-price outlook as the supply keeps building

In its July outlook USDA lowered the 2026 all-milk price forecast to $20.00/cwt while raising the 2026 milk-production forecast to 236.6 billion pounds, citing a larger cow herd and continued strong milk supply; forecast prices for nonfat dry milk, cheese and butter were all trimmed. Why it matters: a lower official price track with more milk on the way is a clear cue to revisit second-half margin protection before the market resets it for you.

03 · Milk Supply

June milk output jumped 2.4% — and the herd is still growing

USDA's monthly report put June milk production at 18.93 billion pounds, up 2.4% from a year earlier, with the milk-cow herd at 9.677 million head (up 192,000 year over year) and output per cow up 7 pounds to 2,051; only 2 of the 24 reporting states saw production fall. Why it matters: herd expansion stacked on higher per-cow yields is the engine behind the price cuts above — milk keeps coming even as processing capacity strains to absorb it.

04 · Stocks

Cold storage flashes a split signal: butter tight, cheese ample

USDA's cold-storage report showed 354.4 million pounds of butter in storage on June 30 — down 3% from May and 6% from a year ago — while total natural cheese held at 1.412 billion pounds, up from May but slightly below last June. Why it matters: lean butter inventories can hold up Class IV and butterfat values even as a milk glut pressures cheese and Class III, a divergence worth weighing before setting class-price hedges.

05 · Inputs & Policy

Fertilizer, pesticides and seeds spared from the new forced-labor tariffs

As tariffs on 60 countries took effect July 24 under the forced-labor trade action, the administration exempted fertilizers, pesticides and seeds — along with bovine products and coffee — after farm groups warned that taxing them would raise production costs; farm equipment remains tariffed. Why it matters: it takes a fresh cost spike off crop-input and forage budgets that set dairy feed costs, though tariffed machinery keeps capital expenses climbing.

06 · Processing

A farmer co-op sells its cheese crown jewels to the world's largest dairy company

Agropur, the large Canadian farmer-owned cooperative, agreed July 15 to sell its fine-cheese division — including the OKA, Monsieur Gustav and L'Extra brands, plants in Oka and Saint-Hyacinthe, and about 400 employees — to Lactalis Canada, pending Competition Bureau approval; terms were undisclosed. Why it matters: a cooperative retreating from value-added cheese to concentrate on core operations, with assets flowing to the global leader, is another data point on consolidation and where processor bargaining power is heading.

07 · Global Markets

Global auction turns higher for the first time in a month

The Global Dairy Trade price index rose 1.5% at the July 21 auction to 1,139 — its first increase after three consecutive declines — with 26,889 tonnes sold to 117 winning bidders. Why it matters: the global reference market ticking up after a losing streak is a modest counterweight to the bearish domestic supply picture and a supportive backdrop for US export pricing.
