September margin lands at $8.35 — DMC pays $1.15 per cwt

USDA's September Dairy Margin Coverage margin came in at $8.35/cwt, built from a $19.50 all-milk price against $11.15/cwt in total feed costs — the heaviest feed burden recorded so far in 2026. Ration components behind it: corn at $4.81/bu, soybean meal at $365.95/ton and dairy-quality alfalfa at $241/ton. Operations covered at the $9.50 level in Tier I collect $1.15/cwt, before the 5.7% sequestration haircut. Why it matters: That is a confirmed payout, not a projection — if you are enrolled at $9.50, budget the $1.15/cwt less sequestration now, and note that it is feed cost, not the milk price, doing the damage this month.

Cheese blocks break 13.75¢ and leave barrels trading above them

Friday's CME spot session knocked 40-lb cheddar blocks down $0.1375 to $1.5975 on three sales, while barrels sat unchanged at $1.7250 with no sales — putting barrels a rare 12.75¢ above blocks. The fat and powder side went the other way: butter rose $0.0375 to $1.3575 on 11 sales, nonfat dry milk added $0.0250 to $2.1125, and dry whey gained $0.02 to $0.84. Why it matters: A block-over-barrel inversion that wide distorts the Class III build and means barrel buyers, not block buyers, are setting your cheese milk price — while strong powder against weak butter keeps Class IV firmer than Class III going into November.

USDA announced it intends to purchase up to $472.5 million in domestic commodities under Section 32 authority, and dairy is the single largest category at $165.5 million — roughly 35% of the package, ahead of pork at $41 million and split peas at $37 million. 04 · Animal Health

Washington goes from zero to 27 H5N1 herds in three weeks

Washington has become the 20th state to detect avian influenza in dairy cattle, and the outbreak is moving fast: from the first two Yakima County herds in early October to 27 positive dairies in the span of three weeks, including a Benton County herd confirmed October 26. Every farm in the targeted milkshed is now being tested weekly. State officials put human risk as very low and note pasteurization inactivates the virus. Why it matters: A naive region went to 27 herds in 21 days, which is the practical argument for tightening visitor, cull-truck and shared-equipment biosecurity now rather than after the first clinical cow — and for expecting weekly bulk-tank obligations if your milkshed is next.

05 · Animal Health

Screwworm reaches a third state as USDA reopens another cattle port

New World screwworm cases in the U.S. have reached 71, with 18 still active, after APHIS confirmed the first Arizona case — a dog, found October 28 — making Arizona the third state with confirmed animal cases after Texas and New Mexico. New Mexico added a dog case October 27. Meanwhile the border keeps opening to cattle: Santa Teresa, NM on September 24, Douglas, AZ on August 24, Columbus, NM on October 26, and Nogales, AZ scheduled for November 30 if inspection conditions hold. Why it matters: Every reopened port raises the odds of an introduction event on the inbound cattle you or your neighbors buy, so any open wound — fresh calvings, dehorning, branding, foot work — needs daily inspection and a treatment plan while the active case count stays in the double digits.

06 · Supply

The September production data shows where the new milk actually is: Kansas, up 14.7%

September output across the 24 major states hit 18.7 billion pounds, up 2.7%, on 9.30 million cows — 162,000 head more than a year ago — and 2,012 pounds per cow. But the national figure hides a reshuffle. Kansas is up 14.7% year over year on 29,000 more cows, South Dakota up 7.9%, Indiana up 7.4% and Georgia up 7.1%, while California is dead flat, Arizona is down 4.9%, Utah down 3.2% and Illinois down 2.2%. Why it matters: Growth is concentrating in the states where new processing capacity went in, which is both the source of the cheese-price pressure you are feeling and a signal about where milk will be short, and basis strong, over the next few years.

08 · Labor

The H-2A wage rule dies December 30, and Labor has to rewrite it first

A federal judge in the Eastern District of California ruled the Labor Department illegally cut H-2A guest worker wages by setting pay well below relevant market rates and skipping public comment. An October 2 order gives the department until December 30 to issue a new wage rule; the current rule expires that day regardless, and if the deadline is missed, rates revert to the prior administration's formula. DOL's own estimate was that the current formula shifted about $2.46 billion a year from workers to employers — in California the minimum H-2A wage fell from $19.97 to $16.90 an hour. H-2A certifications hit a record 398,258 positions in fiscal 2025. Why it matters: Dairies gained limited H-2A access in June 2026 for work that is genuinely temporary or seasonal, so any operation using or pricing that route should plan for a higher wage floor from December 30 — and back pay exposure if retroactivity survives.

09 · Inputs

A meta-analysis of 31 trials finds essential oil additives do nothing measurable

A review in Frontiers in Animal Science pooled 31 eligible reports on plant essential oil feed additives in lactating dairy cows and found no statistically significant effect on any outcome tested. Milk yield came in at SMD 0.47 (95% CI −0.29 to 1.22; P = 0.225), methane emission at SMD −0.87 (P = 0.0835), methane yield at SMD −0.40 (P = 0.498) and methane intensity at SMD −0.79 (P = 0.0748). Heterogeneity was severe throughout, from I² of 85.76% to 95.2%, and the authors call the subgroup patterns hypothesis-generating only. Why it matters: Essential oil products are sold on yield and methane claims this evidence base does not support, so treat any such line item in your ration budget as unproven and demand trial data on your own ration before paying for it.
