The 48-Hour Read for Dairy Operators

Daily Edition · Friday, September 25, 2026

The U.S. herd added another 167,000 cows and still lost ground per cow, so August's 1.7% production gain was bought entirely with headcount. The money, meanwhile, keeps moving toward components and away from volume — while pasture collapses and the cull market quietly tilts dairy's way.

01 · Milk Supply

August milk is up 1.7% — and none of it came from the cow

USDA NASS put August U.S. milk production at 19.9 billion pounds, up 1.7% from a year ago, on 9.71 million cows — 167,000 more than August 2025 and 15,000 more than July. Output per cow averaged 2,049 pounds, one pound below August 2025. Why it matters: Every pound of this year's growth is bought with headcount, not efficiency, so the supply overhang carries the full feed, labor and facility cost of 167,000 extra cows rather than riding free on productivity gains.

02 · Milk Price

October Class I jumps $1.84 — and powder, not cheese, is paying for it

AMS set the October base Class I price at $18.88/cwt, up $1.84 from September. The arithmetic behind it is the story: the advanced Class IV skim factor came in at $14.12/cwt against a Class III skim factor of $11.18 — a $2.94 gap. Because Class I skim takes the higher of the two, nonfat dry milk set the fluid price this month. Two-week averages were butter $1.4678, NDM $1.7731, cheese $1.5598 and dry whey $0.6657. Why it matters: A Class I lift built on the Class IV side does nothing for the cheese-heavy Class III blend most Upper Midwest and Northeast checks ride on, so a headline price increase can arrive with a flat or lower mailbox price.

03 · Cull Market

Dairy is now 10% of the U.S. cattle kill as beef cow slaughter collapses

August federally inspected dairy cow slaughter was 221,100 head against 217,400 a year earlier, while beef cow slaughter fell to 144,300 from 165,100, down 12.6%. Dairy cows made up 10.0% of all cattle slaughtered in August, up from 9.5%. Year to date, dairy cow slaughter is up 4.0% at 1.787 million head even as total cattle slaughter runs 7% below 2025. Why it matters: With beef herds still retaining heifers, the dairy cull is carrying an unusually large share of cow beef supply — which supports cull values now, but means dairy is the first place packers look when they need cows.

04 · Components

CoBank puts a number on the components gap: up to $474 per cow

A CoBank Knowledge Exchange report released Wednesday found herds in the top decile for butterfat and protein earn $101 to $352 more per cow per year at the U.S. average of 24,390 pounds, widening to $136 to $474 per cow at 33,000 pounds. In the Upper Midwest order the advantage worked out to $256.58 per cow at average production and $345.58 at the higher level — about $345,000 a year on a 1,000-cow dairy. The 2025 Upper Midwest averages were 4.37% butterfat, 3.33% protein and 5.79% other solids. Why it matters: More than 80% of farmgate milk now goes into manufactured products and roughly 90% is priced on components, so a half-point of butterfat is worth more per cow than several hundred pounds of additional volume.

05 · Global Supply

Fonterra books a NZ$9.69 season and guides to NZ$9.50 on 1.6 billion kgMS

Fonterra's FY26 results, filed to the NZX on Thursday, confirmed a final 2025/26 Farmgate Milk Price of NZ$9.69 per kgMS and reported profit after tax of NZ$2.6 billion on NZ$27 billion of revenue. For 2026/27 the co-op is forecasting NZ$9.50 per kgMS in a NZ$8.50–$10.50 range, with collections forecast just above 1.6 billion kgMS. Why it matters: A second consecutive season near NZ$9.50 keeps New Zealand suppliers expanding into the same powder and butterfat export channels U.S. plants are counting on to clear surplus solids.

06 · Inputs

Pasture and range hits 53% poor or very poor, against 36% a year ago

NASS rated 48-state pasture and range 25% very poor and 28% poor for the week ending September 20, against 13% and 23% a year ago. Only 17% was good to excellent, down from 34% last year, and the rating deteriorated two points in a single week. The worst readings are in the Northern Plains and Upper Midwest: South Dakota 66% poor or very poor, Nebraska 57%, Minnesota 51%, and Wyoming 67%. Why it matters: Heifer and dry-cow groups that normally graze into November will come onto stored feed early, pulling forward silage and hay draw on inventories that were already short going into fall.

07 · Cattle

August feedlot placements were the lowest for the month since the series began in 1996

Cattle on feed in 1,000-head-plus lots totaled 11.163 million on September 1, up 1% year over year. But August placements fell 9% to 1.617 million head and marketings fell 3% to 1.519 million — both the lowest August figures in the 30-year history of the series. Why it matters: Feedlots that cannot source enough native feeders bid harder for beef-on-dairy calves, which is where a growing share of a dairy's non-milk income now comes from.
